How to use the Rent vs Own Home tool

Buy with a mortgage, or rent and invest the difference? Part 1 shows how to use the tool and its Sensitivity page. Part 2 shows how every figure is worked out. Every demo is the real tool, running live in your browser. Press Pause on one to try it yourself.

    In Quick Start, search a city and pick a home type. Its price, rent, running costs and loan terms fill the form.

Quick Start

A typical home in one of 33 cities, from a studio to a four-bedroom house, with its local price, rent, running costs and loan terms. Each figure and its source is on the Quick Start assumptions page.

On a phone, press Full screen under a demo and turn the phone sideways to read it.

  1. Open the inputs

    On a first visit the card is folded to a rail, so the answer has the room. Click Inputs to open it; it stays the way you leave it.

  2. Search a city

    Type in Search city: a city, its country or its currency. Pick one from the list.

  3. Pick a home

    Home type lists only what that city's market has. The line under it says where the figures come from.

  4. Read the answer

    The form fills and the answer follows at once. There is no Run button.

  • A Quick Start is a fresh start. It resets every field first, so nothing from the last home carries over.
  • Then change anything. The tool keeps your figures in this browser for your next visit.

The Home tab

The home you would buy, the loan you would take, and what owning costs on top of the loan.

  1. Price and deposit

    Property price, then Down payment as a share of it. The amount shows under the slider.

  2. Simple or Detailed

    Simple is one rate, principal and interest. Detailed adds Mortgage type and a Mortgage rate schedule: periods by year of the loan, each Fixed or Floating.

  3. The floating band

    A floating period runs at the middle of its range. The chart shades the low and the high.

  4. Costs of owning

    Setup cost in dollars or as a % of the price, then Ongoing costs of owning a year, month or week, fixed or a % of the home's value.

  • A switch restates the figure. 4.51% of an $855,000 price becomes 38,560.5 dollars, not 4.51 dollars. A change of frequency does the same.
  • Detailed costs. The Detail control above the costs lists each one by name, each with its own basis and growth.
  • Cost = interest only (Detailed) picks what Accumulated cost counts. It never moves net equity.

The Rent tab

The rent you would pay instead, how fast it rises, what renting costs on top, and an optional third path that rents first and buys later.

  1. Rent and its growth

    Rent amount a week, month or year (a switch converts it), then Rent growth a year.

  2. Costs of renting

    Ongoing costs of renting: contents insurance, moving, connections. Fixed, or a % of yearly rent.

  3. Rent-Then-Buy

    Enable Rent-Then-Buy, then Buy at year: the years of renting before it buys.

  4. A third line

    A dashed line joins the chart, changing colour the year it buys, and the answer names where it ends.

  • Rent-Then-Buy buys at the price by then, grown by house price growth, with the same deposit share and a new loan on the same terms.
  • It shares the money. Left blank, the budget and the starting cash grow to fund its later deposit, which lifts Own and Rent by the same amount.

The Assumptions tab

The guesses about the future, and the money both paths start with and live on.

  1. Prices and savings

    General inflation, then Risk-Free Rate (what spare cash earns) and Time horizon.

  2. Property growth

    House price growth (RPPI) and Selling cost. Show tool works the growth out from past prices: Calculate CAGR writes it in.

  3. Cash and the budget

    Initial cash and Monthly housing budget, both optional. Blank, each covers what every path needs.

  4. The lead stays

    A budget moves what each path ends with, not the gap between them.

  • General inflation changes no figure in the plan. It only sets how Show in today's money reads them.
  • Budget annual increase appears once a budget is set, for wage growth or inflation.
  • A plan that runs out of cash is not compared. When a set budget or cash leaves a path below zero, the answer card says which path, from which year, and what to change, and no figures show until it is funded. The Sensitivity page does the same for a column.

The answer and the chart

The sentence at the top is the whole answer. Under it: the money it is read in, four figures and the chart.

  1. The answer

    Which path ends ahead after the horizon and by how much, when owning pulls ahead for good, and each path's net equity.

  2. Today's money

    Show in today's money on divides every figure by general inflation. Off, figures read as the bank account will.

  3. Four figures

    Equity difference, Breakeven year, the Initial cash both paths start with, and the Monthly housing budget range.

  4. The chart

    Net equity, Liquid cash or Accumulated cost. Hover a year to read every line.

  • Blue is owning, gold is renting. Neither is good or bad: they are the two options, so the answer is never coloured as a gain or a loss.
  • Breakeven is for good. It is the year owning gets ahead and stays ahead to the horizon, found from the end backwards.
  • Zoom and pan the chart with the wheel or a drag; ⟳ resets it.

Part 2

How the numbers work

What each field means, where it enters the sums, and what moving it does to the answer and why. All of it is read from the tool's engine (engine.js), which both pages share.

The model in one picture

Two households start with the same cash and spend the same monthly housing budget for the same years. One buys: the deposit and setup costs leave its cash on day one, and the budget pays the mortgage and the owning costs. The other rents: it keeps all its cash and pays the rent and renting costs. Whatever either has left over each month is saved at the Risk-Free Rate. At the horizon the tool asks what each would own if the home were sold.

Own = VT × (1 − s) − BT + CTownnet equity, owning
Rent = CTrentnet equity, renting
Cash each month Cm = Cm−1 × (1 + i)1/12 + (budgetm − outgoingsm)
  • VTthe home's value in the last year
  • sSelling cost, as a share of the sale
  • BTthe loan still owed
  • CTeach path's cash at the horizon
  • ithe Risk-Free Rate a year
  • ma month; budget and outgoings are that month's

The headline, Equity difference, is Own minus Rent. Both paths share the budget and the starting cash, so those cancel out of it, and what is left is the whole decision:

Own − Rent = [VT(1 − s) − BT] − D(1 + i)T − Σ (Om − Rm)(1 + i)(N − m)/12
  • Dthe deposit plus setup costs, paid on day one
  • Omowning's outgoings: repayment plus owning costs
  • Rmrenting's outgoings: rent plus renting costs
  • Nmonths in the horizon, T × 12
  • Opportunity cost. The deposit is not free money: left in the bank it would have grown at the Risk-Free Rate. The middle term is that growth.
  • The monthly gap compounds too. In year 1 owning usually costs more than renting, and the renter saves the difference. As rent grows and the repayment stays level, the gap closes and can turn; after the loan is repaid, owning costs only the running costs.
  • Leverage. The home's growth applies to the whole price, not just the deposit. That is why House price growth moves the answer most.

Budget and Initial cash change how much each path ends with, never which ends ahead or by how much. They decide how much is saved, and both paths save it at the same rate. Set too low, they run a path out of cash, and then the plan is refused rather than compared. The demo on the Assumptions tab shows it: a $6,000 budget lifts both lines and leaves the lead where it was.

Worked example: Perth, two-bedroom house

The Quick Start figures: $855,000 price, 20% deposit, 6.4% over 30 years, 4.51% setup, $7,900 a year to own (growing 2.5%), $650 a week rent (growing 3.5%), $1,400 a year to rent (growing 2.5%), 4.5% house price growth, 2.8% selling cost, 4.6% Risk-Free Rate, 30 years, 2.5% general inflation.

Owning's lead after 30 years

By the identity in the model card: the home's equity, less the deposit and setup invested, less the monthly gap invested.

What moves the answer

Every input moved on its own, from the Perth base case, on the tool's own engine.

  • The answer rests on four guesses about the future: house price growth, how long you stay, rent growth, and the gap between the savings rate and the mortgage rate. A point on a rate, or five years on the horizon, moves it by $150k to $500k.
  • Apart from the rent, the figures you can look up matter far less. Price, deposit, term, running costs, duty and selling costs each move it by less than $50k here. Spend your time on the guesses.
  • Growth cuts both ways, unevenly. A point more adds more than a point less takes away: compounding over 30 years.
  • No single move flips this case. Owning stays ahead in every row, but the breakeven is year 5: sell sooner and the setup and selling costs leave renting ahead.

  1. The base case

    Perth, 2BR house: owning ahead by $850k in today's money.

  2. Growth up a point

    5.5%: the home is worth a third more in year 30. The lead grows by $491k and the breakeven comes in year 3.

  3. Growth down a point

    3.5%: the lead shrinks by $372k, and owning pulls ahead only in year 9.

  4. Back to the base

    4.5% again. Try it yourself: press Pause and drag any slider.

What it leaves out

What the answer rests on, beyond the figures you enter. What this assumes, under the results, lists the ones your plan uses.

  • No tax. None on the gain when the home is sold, none on the interest savings earn, no land tax or rental deductions unless you put them in the running costs.
  • One growth rate, every year. No cycles, no crash, no boom. Your home is one property, not the index its growth came from.
  • The renter's savings earn the Risk-Free Rate. Savings in shares would earn more on average, with risk the tool does not model.
  • The home is sold at the horizon. Net equity takes off the selling cost each year. If you would stay on, that cost is never paid.
  • The loan is plain. No refinancing, offset account, extra repayments, lender's mortgage insurance or fees beyond the setup cost. A floating rate runs at the middle of its range.
  • Rent is renewed forever at rent growth, with no forced moves beyond the renting costs.
  • A shortfall is refused, never borrowed. The mortgage is the only loan, so a set budget or cash that runs a path below zero stops the comparison until it covers every path. The automatic figures always do, except for an interest-only balance falling due at the end of its term: set a budget or initial cash that saves for it.
  • Two rates, two conventions. The repayment uses the mortgage rate ÷ 12, as lenders quote it; savings compound monthly to exactly the Risk-Free Rate over a year.
  • Quick Start figures are local estimates as of the month shown, with sources on the Quick Start assumptions page. General inflation stays at 2.5% for every city: set your own.

Do not lean on it when you may sell near the breakeven year, you cannot defend your growth figure within a point, an interest-only balance falls due that your savings cannot cover, or you compare homes in two currencies without thinking about the exchange rate path.

This guide and the tool are general information, not financial advice. Check the figures that matter to you, and talk to a licensed adviser before you buy.
