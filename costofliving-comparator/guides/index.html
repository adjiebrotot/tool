How to use the Cost of Living Comparator

What would your life cost in another city, and what would you need to earn there to save as much? Part 1 shows how to use the comparator and its Map View. Part 2 shows how every figure is worked out. Every demo is the real tool, running live in your browser. Press Pause on one to try it yourself.

The short version

  1. 1Pick your cities

    A worked example from Quick Start, or a From city and a To city from 500+. The Map View sets your city against all of them.

  2. 2Enter your month

    Your net salary and what you spend, as one figure or row by row: rent, groceries, eating out, utilities and more.

  3. 3Read the answer

    What you would save there, or the net salary you need to earn there to save the same share of pay, or the same amount.

Nothing is uploaded. Every figure is worked out in your browser. This guide is general information, not financial advice.

Quick Start

Six worked examples, each a single professional's month in one home city, net of tax, against its neighbours: Singapore vs Australia, Southeast Asia, US cities, European capitals, Middle East and East Asia. One click opens the Detailed table on I need to earn.

On a phone, press Full screen under a demo and turn the phone sideways to read it.

  1. Pick an example

    Hover a button to read what it holds. A click replaces whatever was on screen.

  2. Read the Net Income row

    The net salary each destination needs to save the same share of pay as home: AUD 10,178 a month in Sydney, AUD 7,174 in Melbourne.

  3. Each row, each city

    Every expense row priced in each city, with what it comes to in the home currency, and the Savings row at the bottom.

  • Round savings ratios. Each budget saves 20, 25, 30 or 40% of pay at home, so the target reads at a glance.
  • Then make it yours. Type over any figure. The examples are a starting point, not a survey of what people earn.

Two cities, one month

Simple mode takes two cities and three figures: your net salary and your spending where you live, and a salary in the other city. It tells you which leaves more at the end of the month.

  1. Pick two cities

    Type in From city and To city and pick from the list. City, country or currency all match.

  2. Enter your month

    Net salary and Expenses, a month, after tax, in your currency. Each shows what it is worth in the other.

  3. And a salary there

    The net salary you would earn in the To city, in its currency.

  4. Read the answer

    The gap in monthly savings in both currencies, and the two savings ratios.

  5. Swap them

    ⇆ turns it round. The salaries change sides, and your expenses become the estimate for the new home city.

  • Estimated monthly expenses is your spending priced in the other city, for the same lifestyle: AUD 2,709 in Perth for IDR 12 million in Jakarta.
  • Two ways to win. More money saved, or a bigger share of pay saved. The sentence says but when the two disagree.

What you need to earn

Switch the question to I need to earn and the To city's salary becomes the answer: the net salary that keeps your savings going after the same lifestyle there.

  1. I need to earn

    The To column now shows Required net monthly salary: AUD 4,515 for this example.

  2. Pick the target

    Savings ratio keeps the same share of pay; Nominal savings keeps the same amount, at today's rate: AUD 3,341.

  3. Housing in or out

    Exclude housing scales your spending by the index without rent, as when an employer pays for your home.

  • A net salary. The answer is take-home pay. To compare it with a job offer, add the tax and social charges of the new country yourself.
  • The two targets can be far apart. Moving to a dearer city, keeping the same share of pay asks for more than keeping the same amount. The reverse holds moving to a cheaper one. Part 2 shows why.

Exchange rates

Between two currencies a card opens under the cities. Money that crosses the border (your salary, your savings, the ≈ figures) moves at today's market rate. The cost estimate does not: shop prices in Perth do not change when the rupiah does.

  1. The rate it uses

    Default is today's rate from a free daily feed, or the bundled rate when the feeds cannot be reached.

  2. Your own rate

    Type the rate you would really get, after a bank's margin. The ≈ figures and the savings gap follow it.

  3. What if AUD moves?

    Move the destination currency up to 20% either way, on top of whichever rate is in force. Reset puts it back.

  4. What does not move

    Estimated monthly expenses stays AUD 2,709, and so does every savings ratio.

  • ISO codes throughout. Every figure carries its currency's three-letter code, so AUD and USD never pass for each other.
  • Rates are kept for six hours in your browser, then asked for again. A feed that disagrees five-fold with the bundled rate is ignored for that currency.

The Detailed table

Detailed mode splits your month into rows and prices each by its own index, in as many destinations as you like. The first column is home.

  1. Add a city

    + City adds a destination column. The bin in its head takes it away.

  2. Add a row

    + Add expense row, then pick its category: thirteen, from rent to remittances. Each ? says how it is priced.

  3. Type over an estimate

    Know the rent you would pay? Type it. The cell turns yellow and replaces the estimate; clear it to go back.

  4. Read the answer

    Net Income at the top, Savings and its ratio at the bottom, per city.

  1. At a custom frequency

    Enter a row per week, fortnight or year, or as a price times a count: per meal, kWh, litre, gallon or trip. Everything is turned into a month.

  2. Move a column

    Drag ⠿ to reorder the cities. The city that lands first becomes home, and every figure keeps its value.

  • FX Rate (custom). A row of its own, one rate per destination in another currency. In this table it does more than in Simple mode; see Exchange rates in Part 2.
  • No data in a cell means the dataset lacks that index for that city. Type your own figure and the totals come back.

Save, open and what it assumes

Each page keeps your comparison in this browser between visits. To keep it elsewhere, save it to a file.

  1. Save or open a file

    The two icons at the end of the Quick Start row write every input to a file, and read one back.

  2. What this assumes

    The last card, opened from its chevron: the index, the salaries, the target and the rate this answer rests on.

  • The Map View saves the same way, and its ⬇ CSV writes every city's figures in both currencies.

Part 2

How the numbers work

What each field means, where it enters the sums, and what moving it does to the answer and why. All of it is read from the tool's own code (script.js, copied function for function into the Map View's).

The model in one picture

The tool never prices your life from scratch. It takes what you spend now and scales it by how much dearer or cheaper each kind of spending is in the other city, according to a price index. Then it asks what salary there keeps your savings target.

Cost there Ed = Σ xi × edeh × Id,iIh,ia month, in the destination's currency
Salary needed Sd = Ed1 − r = Sh × EdEhSavings ratio target
Salary needed Sd = Ed + Sh − EhXNominal savings target
  • xiwhat you spend on row i at home (one row in Simple)
  • Id,i, Ih,ithat row's price index there and at home
  • ed, ehunits of each currency per US dollar, at the rate the indices were priced at
  • Ehyour spending at home, the sum of xi
  • Shyour Net salary at home
  • ryour savings ratio now, (Sh − Eh) ÷ Sh
  • Xtoday's market rate, home currency per 1 of the destination's
  • The exchange rate cancels out of the cost. Each index is a city's prices in US dollars over a base city's, so e × I is a city's prices in its own currency. Ed is a ratio of local prices: it does not move with today's exchange rate.
  • On the savings ratio target, the answer is your salary times one ratio: what your basket costs there over what it costs you now. How much you spend drops out; only the mix of rows matters.
  • On the nominal target, the amount you save is carried across the border at today's rate, so the exchange rate and your spending level both count.

I can save skips the last step: you type the salary there, and savings are Sd − Ed. The sentence compares that with your savings at home converted at X, and the two ratios.

When a ratio of indices misleads

An index prices a typical shopper's basket. Yours is not typical, and the gap shows most in rent.

The same Singapore to Sydney month, three ways

SGD 8,000 net, SGD 6,000 spent, saving 25%. Only the way the spending is priced changes.

  1. Simple, without housingSGD 6,000 × 1.09375 × 102.0 ÷ 105.3, ÷ 0.75AUD 8,476
  2. Simple, with housingSGD 6,000 × 1.09375 × 110.6 ÷ 106.5, ÷ 0.75AUD 9,087
  3. Detailed, five rowsThe worked example aboveAUD 10,178
  • The overall index weights rent like a typical household. This budget puts 58% into rent, and Sydney's rent index is 20% above Singapore's while the overall index with housing is only 4% above. Simple mode misses AUD 1,091 a month.
  • A city is not one price. The rent index is a median across a city's neighbourhoods. Your street may sit far from it.
  • Prices are not choices. The estimate keeps your lifestyle fixed. People who move change it: they cook more where eating out is dear, or rent smaller where rent is. The answer is the cost of not adapting.
  • A survey, not a census. The indices are crowd-sourced and thin in some cities. Where you know a real figure, type it over the estimate.

Use Detailed with your own rent as a row whenever rent is a large or unusual share of your month.

What moves the answer

Each input moved on its own, from the Singapore vs Australia base case, on the tool's own engine.

  • Two things decide it: your pay and how right the prices there are. Each moves the answer one for one. Of the prices, rent is the piece worth checking: a 10% error in the Sydney rent is AUD 610 a month.
  • How much you spend does not matter on this target. Spend 10% more at home and you save less, so the share to keep falls by just enough. Only the mix moves it: more on a row dearer in Sydney (rent, eating out) lifts it a little, more on a cheaper one (other) lowers it.
  • The exchange rate does not touch it. Every figure in this answer is in Australian dollars, priced in Australian shops. On the nominal target it would: AUD 9,821 becomes AUD 10,064 with the Singapore dollar 10% stronger, and AUD 9,622 with it 10% weaker.
  • So for a decision: negotiate on the salary needed, but check the rent you would really pay before you trust it, and remember the answer is net of tax.

  1. The base case

    SGD 8,000 net, saving 25%: Sydney needs AUD 10,178.

  2. Salary up 10%

    SGD 8,800, spending unchanged, now saving 31.8%. Every destination needs 10% more: Sydney AUD 11,196.

  3. Salary down 10%

    SGD 7,200, saving 16.7%. Sydney needs AUD 9,161.

  4. Rent up 10%, salary back

    SGD 3,850 rent on SGD 8,000: you save less, so the bar barely moves. Sydney AUD 10,194.

What it leaves out

What the answer rests on, beyond the figures you enter. What this assumes, under the results, lists the ones your comparison uses.

  • No tax. Salaries are net, as you type them. Nothing grosses a salary up through income tax or social charges, so a required salary is take-home pay, not an offer.
  • One lifestyle, fixed. No household size, no lifestyle level, no change of habits on arrival. A family is priced as whatever its month already costs.
  • Prices from a survey. Crowd-sourced indices, updated May 2026; health care from the World Bank ICP 2021 by country. A city lacking an index shows no estimate rather than a guess.
  • One month, today. No inflation from here, no change in rents or rates over time, no one-off costs of moving, deposits or visas.
  • Public services left out. Schooling, health cover and transport paid through tax differ widely; the tool prices only what you pay directly.
  • Exchange rates from a free feed, with no bank margin unless you type your own rate.
  • Scaled pay is rough. Average salaries are crowd-sourced; a median comes from one national ratio; minimum wages are gross.

Do not lean on it when rent is a big share of your month and you have not typed your own, you compare gross offers, your household will change with the move, or the city's figures come from a thin survey.

This guide and the tool are general information, not financial advice. Check the figures that matter to you before you accept an offer or move.
